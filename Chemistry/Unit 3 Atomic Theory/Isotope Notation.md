Isotope notation uses the following format, represented with
**A** =  Mass number
**Z** = Atomic number
**X** = Symbol
$$\ce{^A_ZX}$$
Isotopes can also be written as <sup>mass</sup>Element or Element-mass. So for the element **Lithium** with a mass of **7**, it can be written as either, <sup>7</sup>Li, Li-7, or $\ce{^7_3Li}$
## Determining Protons, Neutrons, and Electrons
Let's say we have $$\ce{^{10}_5B}$$
Since the $5$ is the atomic number, we already know protons and electrons. We know that this element has **5** protons and **5** electrons. And $10$ is the atomic mass, which is **protons + neutrons**. So we can just subtract the atomic mass from the atomic number, which gets us **5** neutrons.
## Cations and Anions
If you check the [[Periodic Table]], many columns fall under a common charge. For example, nitrogen has a common charge of 3- on the periodic table. To write that on isotope notation, it is $$\ce{^{12}_7N^{3-}}$$
Negative charges mean more electrons and positive charges mean less electrons. A neutral charge (or nothing to the right of the element) means number of protons and neutrons are **equal**. This means that the atomic number 6 has a caveat. We need to add 3 to the number of electrons from 6, meaning there are 6 protons and 9 electrons in this nitrogen atom.
