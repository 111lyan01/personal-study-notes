## Polarization and Gridlock
Over time, both political parties of the United States became more cohesive, with republicans citing as conservatives and democrats as liberals. This plus gerrymandered district maps adds to increasing **polarization** as each party caters more to its audience and builds opposition against the other parties. **Divided government** can become a result of this. A divided government with no compromise can lead to stalled legislative activity, known as **Gridlock**.
## "Crossing Over"
Some Congressmen may vote against their party. This can be due to a number of matters, such as benefiting their constituents and their personal beliefs. Members are also often provided cues and information by party leadership or trusted colleagues, since they can't know everything.
## Logrolling, Earmarks, Pork
Leaders often offer incentives to get their legislation passed. Congressmen want to support their constituents by "bringing home the bacon," which means passing bills that would benefit the regions they represent. **Logrolling** is when a member trades their vote on a particular bill with another member in exchange for their vote. Kinda like a, "you vote for me, I vote for you." Members also sometimes ask for special appropriations for projects back home to be attached to a bill to gain votes. This is known as **earmark.** Special projects designed to benefit constituents are known as **pork, or pork barrel.**
## How Bills Become Laws
Bills are introduced in either the House or the Senate. Similar bills are usually introduced in both chambers. **Money bills must start in the House.** The bill is then referred to a committee and its subcommittees for study, discussion, and revision. Once reported out to the full chamber, it is scheduled for debate by the Rules Committee in the House and by the leadership in the Senate, and when passed in both houses, its modified in the conference committees, approved by both the House and the Senate, and sent to the president.
